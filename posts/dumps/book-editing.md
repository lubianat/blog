# Book Editing: How Books Are Shepherded from Idea to Libraries

## TL;DR
- A traditionally published book moves through a long relay of specialists—agent, acquiring editor, developmental editor, copyeditor, designer, proofreader, indexer, production staff, and library suppliers—and typically takes **12 to 24 months from signed contract to bookstore and library shelves**, on top of the months (or years) spent writing and finding a publisher.
- The work moves from **big questions early to small corrections late**: structural rethinking is welcome at the start, but once a book is typeset into pages, changes become slow and expensive—so expensive that contracts make authors pay for excessive last-minute edits (typically anything above **10–15% of the typesetting cost**).
- Getting a book "into libraries" is its own machinery: cataloguing programs (the Library of Congress CIP program), identifiers (ISBNs), legal deposit (e.g., the British Library), advance review copies sent to journals like *Library Journal*, and library wholesalers that acquisitions librarians order from.

## Key Findings
- **The costliest thing in publishing is a late change.** Every stage narrows what kind of edit is welcome. Rewrite the ending during developmental editing and everyone applauds; rewrite it in page proofs and you may repaginate the book, redo the index, and trigger a bill.
- **"Editing" is not one job.** It is at least four different jobs done by different people at different times: developmental (structure), line (flow and voice), copyediting (correctness and consistency), and proofreading (catching errors in the typeset pages).
- **Academic and university presses differ mainly at the front and back ends:** they add formal peer review and an editorial board vote before a contract, and they lean on authors to supply (and often pay for) the index.
- **The library supply chain is a distinct world** with its own reviewers, identifiers, and middlemen—and it is currently being reshaped by the collapse of Baker & Taylor, long the largest U.S. library book wholesaler, which began winding down in late 2025.

## Details

### Stage 0: The Idea, the Proposal, and the Literary Agent
Most trade books begin not with a finished manuscript but with a pitch. For nonfiction, that pitch is usually a **book proposal** (an outline, sample chapters, market analysis, and author bio). For fiction, it is usually a finished novel plus a **query letter**—a one-page sales pitch.

The gatekeeper here is the **literary agent**: a professional who represents the writer to publishers, much like a talent agent represents an actor. Agents know which editors buy which kinds of books, negotiate the contract, and protect the author's interests. Crucially, they are paid on commission, not upfront fees. The Association of American Literary Agents (AALA) Canon of Ethics sets the norm at **typically 15% for U.S. sales and 20–25% for foreign, translation, or film/TV rights** (foreign and film deals are higher because they are split with co-agents), and it prohibits reading fees—AALA agents may not charge clients to read or evaluate manuscripts. Treat any "agent" who wants money upfront as a red flag.

**How long:** Querying agents can take weeks to many months; being "on submission" to publishers averages **2–12 months**. This whole phase sits *before* the contract.

**Changes welcome here:** Everything. This is the blank-slate stage. Agents often ask for revisions before they'll send a book out.

### Stage 1: Acquisition
When an editor wants to buy a book, they can't just say yes. The **acquiring editor** (also called the acquisitions or commissioning editor) is the book's in-house champion—the person who falls in love with the project and then has to convince colleagues to bet money on it. Think of them as the book's sponsor inside the company.

To get approval, the editor takes the project to an **acquisitions meeting** (or editorial meeting): a regular gathering, often weekly, where editors pitch prospective books to colleagues from sales, marketing, and management. The editor prepares a **P&L (profit-and-loss statement)**—a spreadsheet estimating what the book will cost to produce and what it will earn—often using sales of comparable titles as a guide. If the group agrees, the house makes an offer.

Then comes the **contract**, negotiated by the agent. It sets the **advance** (money paid upfront against future royalties), royalty rates, delivery deadlines, rights, and the clauses that govern editing and author's alterations (more on those below). Getting from a verbal "yes" to a signed contract commonly takes **1–4 months** just for the paperwork.

**How university/academic presses differ:** Here the process is more rigorous and slower. The manuscript (or proposal) goes out for **peer review**—evaluation by other scholars in the field. The acquiring editor then presents the project, the reader reports, and the author's response to a **faculty editorial board**, which must approve the book before a contract is finalized. As Princeton University Press tells its authors, "All PUP books with a standard contract must be approved by the Editorial Board." This is the biggest structural difference from trade publishing.

### Stage 2: Developmental (Structural) Editing and the Editorial Letter
This is the "big questions" stage. **Developmental editing** (also called structural or substantive editing) addresses the book at the largest scale: Is the argument sound? Does the plot work? Is the structure right? Scott Norton, in his standard handbook *Developmental Editing*, describes it as "significant structuring or restructuring of a manuscript's discourse." In *What Editors Do*, his chapter on the subject carries the memorable title "Open-Heart Surgery or Just a Nip and Tuck?"

The editor's main deliverable is the **editorial letter**: a multi-page memo (often 10–15 pages) laying out what's working, what isn't, and how to fix it. It is a holdover from the days when editors literally mailed their notes back with the manuscript. It reads as guidance, not commands—big-picture notes on character arcs, pacing, organization, and logic, usually paired with in-line comments in the manuscript itself.

**Who's involved:** At large houses, the acquiring editor often does this work; elsewhere a dedicated developmental editor does.

**How long:** Weeks to a few months for the edit; then the author revises.

**Changes welcome:** The most sweeping ones. Cut a character, reorder chapters, change the thesis—now is the time. Nothing has been typeset, so structural change costs only effort.

### Stage 3: Revision Rounds
The author takes the editorial letter and rewrites. There may be one round or several, cycling between author and editor until the manuscript is judged **"accepted"**—a contractual milestone that often triggers a payment and starts the production clock. In-house editors historically could do "as many rounds as needed," focusing on big structural issues first and smaller ones later.

**Changes welcome:** Still substantial, but narrowing. By the last revision round, you're refining rather than rebuilding.

### Stage 4: Line Editing
**Line editing** works at the level of the sentence and paragraph—rhythm, word choice, clarity, tone, and voice. If developmental editing asks "should this chapter exist?", line editing asks "is this sentence doing its job?" In *What Editors Do* (Peter Ginna, ed., University of Chicago Press, 2017), George Witte's chapter on line editing is aptly titled "This Just Needs a Little Work."

Line editing and copyediting overlap and are sometimes combined, but line editing is about *craft and style* while copyediting is about *correctness and consistency*.

**Changes welcome:** Sentence-level rewriting, tightening, smoothing. Not restructuring.

### Stage 5: Copyediting
The **copyeditor** is the meticulous mechanic. They go through every word and mark of punctuation, fixing grammar, spelling, and punctuation; enforcing consistency (is it "email" or "e-mail"? is the character's name spelled the same way throughout?); checking obvious facts; and flagging problems. Carol Fisher Saller's chapter in *What Editors Do*, "Toward Accuracy, Clarity, and Consistency: What Copyeditors Do," captures the goal in its title. Several tools and concepts appear here:

- **Queries:** Polite questions the copyeditor leaves for the author in the margins—"Do you mean 1998 or 1999?"—rather than silently changing the text.
- **Style sheet:** A document the copyeditor builds *for this specific book*, recording every decision (spellings, hyphenation, character details, treatment of numbers) so everyone downstream stays consistent.
- **House style:** The publisher's default rulebook for mechanics—not writing style, but conventions like how to format numbers and dates.
- **The Chicago Manual of Style:** The dominant style guide for U.S. book publishing. Its Chapter 2 covers manuscript editing and proofreading and lays out the whole process. When a publisher's house style is silent, editors "follow Chicago."
- **Stet:** Latin for "let it stand"—a mark meaning "ignore that edit; keep the original." Authors and editors use it to protect wording an edit would have changed.
- **Fact-checking, legal review, and permissions:** Depending on the book, this stage may include verifying facts, a **legal (libel) review** by a lawyer who reads for defamation, invasion of privacy, and similar risks, and clearing **permissions** for quoted song lyrics, poetry, images, or long excerpts. Legal vetting "does not copyedit" and "does not fact check"—it is a separate risk review, often run in parallel.

**Who's involved:** A copyeditor (often freelance) managed by a production or project editor; the author reviews and answers queries.

**How long:** A few weeks for the edit, plus author review time.

**Changes welcome:** Corrections and consistency fixes. This is the last comfortable moment for the author to tweak wording. After this, the text is "locked" and poured into a design.

### Stage 6: Design and Typesetting (Composition)
Now the words become a book object. **Typesetting** (also called **composition**) is the process of flowing the edited text into a designed page layout—choosing fonts, margins, spacing, chapter openers—so it looks like a printed book rather than a word-processor file. A designer creates the interior template and the cover; a compositor (in-house or a vendor) sets the pages. The person quarterbacking all of this in-house is usually the **production editor** (sometimes called project editor or, one rung up, **managing editor**): per the Association of University Presses' "Ask UP" service, once a manuscript is final "a production editor (PE) takes over as the in-house shepherd of the project," responsible for "preparing it for copyediting; hiring and managing freelance copyeditors... transmitting the manuscript for design and typesetting; sending layout proofs to the author... and maintaining house style guides." In trade houses the managing editor typically supervises a team of production editors and the overall production schedule.

This is the hinge of the whole process. **Before** typesetting, changing text is cheap—it's just editing a file. **After** typesetting, every change means someone must re-flow pages, and a change on page 10 can shove text onto different pages all the way to the end, breaking cross-references, the table of contents, and the index. This is precisely why the cost of change rises so sharply from here on.

**How long:** Weeks.

### Stage 7: Proofs, Galleys, Proofreading, and Indexing
Once typeset, the book is output as **proofs**—test versions used to catch errors before printing.

- **Galleys / first pass pages:** The first typeset version. Historically "galleys" meant long unpaginated columns; today the term often overlaps with the **first pass pages**—the first look at the book in its real page layout.
- **Second pass pages:** A corrected version incorporating fixes from the first pass, checked again to confirm nothing broke.

The **proofreader** now enters—a fresh set of eyes who compares the typeset pages against the edited manuscript and hunts for typos, layout glitches, bad line breaks, and anything the copyedit missed. Crucially, the proofreader is *not* re-editing; they are catching errors in the set pages. As style guides stress, edits at this stage must shrink to fixing genuine errors, because changes are now costly to make and track.

**Indexing** also happens here, and it *must* wait for pages, because an index points to page numbers. A professional **indexer** (or the author) works from the page proofs in a tight window. As the *Chicago Manual of Style* puts it, "Most book indexes must be assembled swiftly between the time page proofs are issued and the time they are returned to the typesetter—usually about four weeks." For nonfiction and nearly all academic books, the index is the author's responsibility, and university presses often make the author pay for professional indexing (deductible from royalties) if they don't do it themselves.

**Changes welcome:** Error corrections only. Rewriting is strongly discouraged—and may cost the author money.

### Stage 8: Author's Alterations—Why Late Changes Cost Money
Publishing contracts formalize the "changes get expensive" rule with an **author's alterations (AAs)** clause. The deal is: the publisher pays to fix its own (and the typesetter's) errors, but if the *author* makes discretionary changes to the typeset proofs beyond a set threshold, the author pays for the excess—usually deducted from royalties.

The threshold is customarily expressed as a percentage of the original typesetting/composition cost. Real examples:
- The **SFWA Model Hardcover Contract (1989)** sets it at **15%**: "The cost of alterations in the galley proof or page proof required by the Author, other than corrections of printer's errors, in excess of fifteen percent (15%) of the original cost of composition, shall be charged against the earnings of the Author."
- A widely used **St. Martin's-style** clause sets it at **10%**: "The cost of the Author's alterations in the proofs in excess of ten percent (10%) of the initial setting cost will be charged against the Author's royalty account, except that the Author will not be charged for typesetter's errors."
- The **Authors Guild** model translator contract uses the same mechanism, with thresholds of 10–15% of the cost of typesetting.

The lesson for newcomers: the contract itself encodes the principle that change gets costlier as the book advances. Fix it in the manuscript, not in the proofs.

### Stage 9: Advance Reader Copies (ARCs)
Months before publication, the publisher prints **advance reader copies** (ARCs, also called galleys, bound galleys, or advance review copies)—early, not-fully-final versions of the book sent out to generate reviews and buzz. They typically carry a warning that the text isn't final and shouldn't be quoted.

Who gets them: trade review journals (*Publishers Weekly*, *Kirkus Reviews*, *Library Journal*, *Booklist*), booksellers, librarians, bloggers, and media. These are sent early—commonly **four to six months** before release—because the journals review months ahead so libraries and stores can plan orders. Increasingly ARCs are digital (e-galleys, often via NetGalley).

This stage matters enormously for libraries: those trade-journal reviews are a primary input acquisitions librarians use to decide what to buy.

### Stage 10: Cataloguing for Libraries
Several systems make a book findable and orderable by libraries worldwide.

- **ISBN (International Standard Book Number):** The unique product code identifying a specific title, edition, and format. Every format (hardcover, paperback, ebook, audio) gets its own ISBN. In the U.S., ISBNs come from **Bowker**, the official agency. Without one, a book is nearly invisible to the supply chain.
- **Library of Congress Cataloging in Publication (CIP) program:** The Library of Congress creates a catalogue record *before* the book is published; the publisher prints it on the copyright page (the "CIP data block"), and the Library distributes the record to libraries and vendors. The purpose is "to serve the nation's libraries by cataloging books in advance of publication." Eligibility is restricted: per the LC's membership requirements, "Every publisher/imprint must have already published a minimum of three titles by three different authors," and "All three titles must have been acquired by at least 1,000 U.S. libraries"—which CIP staff verify by searching WorldCat. "Authors and editors who pay for or subsidize publication of their own works are ineligible."
- **PCN (Preassigned Control Number):** For publishers who don't qualify for CIP, this program assigns a Library of Congress Control Number in advance so libraries can catalogue the book. (CIP and PCN are mutually exclusive.)
- **Legal deposit:** The law in many countries requiring publishers to send copies of everything they publish to national libraries for preservation. In the UK, under the Legal Deposit Libraries Act 2003, "The British Library Board is entitled to delivery... of a copy of every work published in print," and "the copy must be delivered within one month beginning with the day of publication." Five other legal deposit libraries (the Bodleian, Cambridge University Library, the National Libraries of Scotland and Wales, and Trinity College Dublin) may request a copy within twelve months, usually via the Agency for Legal Deposit Libraries. UK legal deposit has existed since 1662.

**How academic presses differ:** They live and die by library sales, so accurate CIP data, correct ISBNs, and legal deposit are treated as essential, not optional.

### Stage 11: Printing, Distribution, and Getting into Libraries
The corrected files go to the **printer**; bound books ship to the publisher's warehouse and then out to the trade. Getting from files to books in the warehouse commonly takes **one to two months**, with books reaching stores a week or two after.

Libraries generally don't buy directly from publishers. They buy through **library wholesalers/distributors**—middlemen who stock titles from thousands of publishers, offer library-specific services (cataloguing, protective binding, "shelf-ready" processing), and let a library order everything on one account. For decades the dominant U.S. player was **Baker & Taylor**; **Ingram Content Group** is the other giant. This landscape is in upheaval: in October 2025, Baker & Taylor—an almost two-century-old firm serving more than 4,000 institutional customers—told staff at an October 6 town hall that leadership saw no sustainable path to keep the library wholesaler in business. It laid off hundreds of employees and moved to wind down by the beginning of 2026, pushing thousands of libraries to open new accounts with Ingram and smaller wholesalers such as Bookazine and Brodart.

The decision-makers on the library side are **acquisitions/collection-development librarians**. They select titles based heavily on reviews in the trade journals (*Library Journal*, *Publishers Weekly*, *Kirkus*, *Booklist*), anticipated patron demand, and standing orders—then place orders through their wholesaler, often 3–4 months ahead of publication.

### Stage 12: After Publication—Corrections, Errata, and New Editions
The book is out, but the text isn't necessarily frozen forever.

- **Reprint corrections:** When the first printing sells out and the publisher orders a **reprint** (a new print run of the same edition), small fixes—typos, a broken URL—can be quietly folded in. Publishers keep a "corrections file" for exactly this. Minor corrections keep the same ISBN and don't make it a new edition.
- **Errata:** A separately published list of significant errors and their corrections, used when a fix can't wait for a reprint (common in academic and technical books).
- **Revised / new editions:** When the changes are substantial—new chapters, updated data, major revisions—the book becomes a **new edition**, which gets a new ISBN, a new edition statement, and is treated as a new product. In the UK, a new edition triggers legal deposit afresh.

## Recommendations
For a newcomer trying to work *with* this process rather than against it:

1. **Front-load your big changes.** Do your ambitious rethinking during developmental editing and revision rounds, when change is free. If you have doubts about structure, raise them before copyediting, not after.
2. **Treat "accepted manuscript" and "typesetting" as points of no easy return.** Once the text is copyedited and set into pages, discipline yourself to fix only genuine errors. This protects both the schedule and your wallet.
3. **Read your author's alterations clause before you sign.** Know your threshold (commonly 10–15% of composition cost) and ask the publisher to itemize any AA charges. Benchmark: if you find yourself wanting to change more than roughly a tenth of the set pages, you left revisions too late.
4. **If you're self-publishing or with a small press, don't skip the library plumbing.** Buy proper ISBNs (one per format), get a PCN if you can't get CIP, and comply with legal deposit. These are cheap and decisive for library discoverability.
5. **Court the review journals early.** Because acquisitions librarians rely on trade reviews and order months ahead, get ARCs out four to six months before publication. Missing that window can mean missing the library buying cycle entirely.
6. **For academic authors:** budget time and money for peer review, the editorial-board vote, and your index. These are the steps most likely to surprise (and delay) first-time scholarly authors.

**Typical overall timeline (signed contract → publication):** roughly **12 to 24 months** for trade books, broken down approximately as: delivery and editing (developmental, line, copyediting) up to ~a year; design, typesetting, and proofs a few months; ARCs and reviews four to six months before release; printing and distribution one to two months. Academic books often run longer because of peer review. This is *after* the querying/submission phase, which can add many more months.

## Caveats
- **Ranges vary widely.** Timelines and stage lengths differ by publisher, genre, book length, and season. The figures here are typical, not guaranteed; illustrated children's books, complex nonfiction, and academic monographs can run much longer.
- **Roles blur.** At small presses one person may do acquisitions, developmental editing, and project management; at large houses these are distinct jobs. Titles like "production editor," "project editor," and "managing editor" overlap and are used differently across houses.
- **Some sources are practitioner blogs and vendor pages.** Where possible this piece relies on primary sources (Library of Congress, British Library, the Chicago Manual, model contracts, university-press author pages). Anecdotal timelines from author blogs are indicative rather than authoritative.
- **The library-distribution landscape is in flux.** The Baker & Taylor wind-down is recent and its full effects on library ordering are still settling as of early 2026.
- **Terminology conflicts exist.** "Galley" is used loosely to mean both rough early proofs and near-final ARCs; "line editing" and "copyediting" are sometimes merged. Expect variation in the wild.

## Glossary
- **Acquiring (acquisitions/commissioning) editor:** The editor who champions a book inside a publisher and shepherds it through approval and editing.
- **Advance:** Money paid to the author upfront, recouped against future royalties.
- **ARC (advance reader copy / galley):** An early, not-final copy sent out before publication to generate reviews and buzz.
- **Author's alterations (AAs):** Author-requested changes to typeset proofs; beyond a contractual threshold, the author pays for them.
- **CIP (Cataloging in Publication):** A Library of Congress catalogue record created before publication and printed in the book.
- **Composition / typesetting:** Flowing edited text into a designed page layout.
- **Copyediting:** Sentence-level correction for grammar, consistency, and mechanics.
- **Developmental (structural) editing:** Big-picture editing of structure, argument, or plot.
- **Editorial letter:** The developmental editor's multi-page memo of feedback.
- **First/second pass pages:** The first and corrected typeset versions of the book.
- **House style:** A publisher's in-house rulebook for mechanics.
- **Imprint:** A brand name under which a publisher issues books.
- **ISBN:** The unique identifier for a specific book title, edition, and format.
- **Legal deposit:** The legal duty to send published copies to national libraries.
- **Line editing:** Editing for sentence-level flow, clarity, and voice.
- **Literary agent:** The author's representative to publishers, paid ~15% commission.
- **Managing/production editor:** In-house staff who run a book through production—scheduling, copyediting, typesetting, and proofs.
- **PCN:** A preassigned Library of Congress Control Number for publishers ineligible for CIP.
- **Peer review:** Scholarly evaluation used by academic presses before acquisition.
- **Permissions:** Clearances to reuse third-party material (lyrics, images, long quotes).
- **Proofreading:** Catching errors in the typeset pages (not re-editing).
- **Proofs / galleys:** Test versions of the typeset book used to catch errors.
- **Stet:** "Let it stand"—a mark to reverse an edit and keep the original.
- **Style sheet:** The copyeditor's per-book log of spelling and style decisions.
- **Wholesaler/distributor:** A middleman that supplies books (and services) to libraries and stores.

*A closing thought:* the whole journey runs in one direction—from the biggest questions to the smallest marks. Early on, an editor can help you rethink what the book is *about*; by the end, the work is comma placement and page breaks. And as the book advances, the cost of change rises with it—cheap in a Word file, expensive in typeset pages, and contractually billed once you cross the threshold. Understanding that gradient is the single most useful thing a first-time author can carry into the process.
